Hospitality Technology Capability Assessment

Objective: Explore the supplied webpage or application thoroughly and produce an evidence-based assessment of its capabilities, operational relevance and potential contribution to a wider hospitality technology ecosystem.

The assessment must go beyond describing the interface. Understand what the service does, how it works, what information it handles, which operational problems it addresses and how it could relate to other services in a multi-site hospitality organisation such as The Inn Collection Group.

1. Explore the Service

Access the supplied URL and investigate the available interface, pages, modules and workflows.

* Navigate through the application wherever possible.  
* Interact with menus, buttons, filters, forms, dashboards and other available features.  
* Inspect underlying source code or available technical documentation when accessible.  
* Identify the service’s purpose, structure, intended users and principal functionality.  
* Record inaccessible areas or features that cannot be tested.

Do not limit the assessment to the landing page.

2. Identify and Document Capabilities

For each significant capability, explain:

* What it does.  
* How it works.  
* What information it uses or produces.  
* Which users or operational roles could benefit.  
* What practical hospitality problem it addresses.

Identify relevant applications across areas such as:

* F\&B and menu management.  
* Recipes, ingredients and product specifications.  
* Suppliers, procurement and inventory.  
* Pricing, costing and commercial performance.  
* Allergens, nutrition and compliance.  
* Site operations and coordination.  
* Staff communication, training and knowledge.  
* Management information and decision-making.

Only include areas genuinely relevant to the service.

3. Establish Evidence and Implementation Status

Every identified capability must be classified as one of the following:

* Observed: Directly tested through interaction with the application.  
* Code-verified: Confirmed through source code or technical documentation.  
* Inferred: Suggested by the available evidence but not verified.  
* Proposed: A potential future capability requiring development or integration.

Clearly distinguish working functionality from sample data, mock-ups, demonstrations and intended behaviour.

Never claim an integration, automation, persistence mechanism or operational outcome without evidence.

4. Understand the Information Architecture

Identify:

* The primary information entities (e.g. products, dishes, menus, suppliers, locations, employees or transactions).  
* Their relationships and dependencies.  
* Data sources, formats, inputs and outputs.  
* Whether information is live, static, manually maintained, imported or calculated.  
* How information is updated, stored, validated and shared.  
* What operational consequences might follow when information changes.

Consider how the service could interact with other operational systems without assuming that integrations already exist.

5. Assess Hospitality and Multi-Site Applications

Evaluate the service in the context of a hospitality group operating multiple locations.

Consider how it could support:

* Central coordination and local execution.  
* Consistency across sites while allowing legitimate local variations.  
* Information accessibility for different operational roles.  
* Communication between central teams, management and individual sites.  
* Reduction of duplicated work and disconnected information.  
* Operational visibility, commercial control and decision-making.

Explain practical examples rather than making generic claims about efficiency or productivity.

6. Assess AI and Automation Opportunities

Identify opportunities to enhance the service through advanced AI, automation or connection with other systems.

Consider:

* Natural-language access to operational information.  
* Context-aware assistance for employees.  
* Information extraction, classification and validation.  
* Workflow automation and decision support.  
* Predictive analysis and anomaly detection.  
* Connections between previously disconnected datasets and services.

Distinguish existing AI functionality from opportunities requiring future development.

Consider the Olivia ecosystem principle:

“Software always asks people to learn software. Olivia asks software to learn people.”

Focus on how technology could adapt to existing human workflows, responsibilities and operational contexts rather than requiring employees to learn another complicated system.

7. Final Assessment Structure

Produce a structured report containing:

1. Service Identification: Name, URL, purpose and general description.  
     
2. Interface and Architecture: Main components, modules and navigation.  
     
3. Capability Map: Functionalities, explanations and implementation status.  
     
4. Observed Workflows: Important interactions and operational journeys.  
     
5. Information Architecture: Entities, relationships, data sources and outputs.  
     
6. Hospitality Applications: Practical relevance to individual sites and central operations.  
     
7. AI and Automation Opportunities: Potential enhancements and integrations.  
     
8. Limitations and Dependencies: Technical, operational and data-related constraints.  
     
9. Ecosystem Contribution: How this service could complement other components of a wider hospitality environment.  
     
10. Further Investigation: Important unanswered questions and evidence required.  
      
11. Reporting Standards  
      
* Be technically accurate, analytical and specific.  
* Prioritise meaningful capabilities over superficial interface descriptions.  
* Use concrete examples from the actual application.  
* Distinguish evidence, interpretation and recommendations.  
* Identify contradictions or inconsistencies in the implementation.  
* Do not invent features, integrations, data sources or business relationships.  
* Do not modify, delete, submit or publish anything.  
* Do not assume that public information is equivalent to verified internal operational data.

The final report should be sufficiently detailed to serve as a standalone technical and operational reference, while remaining understandable to hospitality professionals, managers and technology stakeholders.

Ultimately, establish what this component contributes, what information it makes available, which people and processes it serves, and how it could participate in a larger, interconnected, human-centred hospitality technology ecosystem.
